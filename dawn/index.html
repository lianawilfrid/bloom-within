Eating disorders can look like anything

They affect people of every age, gender, body size, race and background. You don't have to "look sick" to deserve help.

Anorexia

Restricting food, an intense fear of weight gain, and a distorted view of one's own body — at any body size.

Bulimia

Cycles of eating large amounts and then trying to compensate, often hidden behind shame and secrecy.

Binge eating disorder

Repeated episodes of eating with a feeling of loss of control, often followed by distress. It's the most common eating disorder.

ARFID

Avoiding or restricting foods because of sensory issues, fear of choking or low interest in eating — not because of body image.

OSFED

Serious eating disorders that don't fit one neat category. Just as real, and just as deserving of treatment.

Signs worth gently noticing

  • Constant preoccupation with food, eating or body shape
  • Skipping meals with others, or eating in secret
  • Rigid rules or rituals around food
  • Mood changes, anxiety or irritability around meals
  • Pulling away from friends and things once enjoyed

Some things we've been told aren't true

Tap a myth to cross it out.

  1. They're complex illnesses shaped by biology, psychology and environment. Nobody chooses one.

  2. Most people with eating disorders are not visibly underweight. Size tells you nothing about suffering.

  3. People of every gender, age, culture and background are affected — including men, older adults and children.

  4. If it's affecting your life, it's serious enough. You don't need to be "sick enough" to reach out.

  5. Many people fully recover. With support, healing is possible at any stage.

  6. Food is often where distress shows up. Underneath can be anxiety, control, trauma, grief or identity.

When it's someone you love

Start with care, not food

Pick a calm, private moment. Use "I" statements: "I've noticed you seem stressed around meals, and I care about you." Talk about feelings and behaviour you've seen, not weight or appearance.

Expect resistance — and stay

Denial, anger or secrecy are common. That doesn't mean you failed. Let them know the door stays open and you're not going anywhere.

Leave body and diet talk behind

Skip comments on anyone's size, "good" or "bad" foods, and compliments about weight loss. Praise who they are, not how they look.

Encourage professional help

Offer to help find a doctor, therapist or dietitian experienced in eating disorders — or to sit with them while they call a helpline.

Look after yourself too

Supporting someone is heavy. Carer communities like F.E.A.S.T. and Beat's carer services exist for you as well.
