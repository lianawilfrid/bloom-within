Notice 5 things you can see

Eating disorders can look like anything

They affect people of every age, gender, body size, race and background. You don't have to "look sick" to deserve help.

Anorexia

Restricting food, an intense fear of weight gain, and a distorted view of one's own body — at any body size.

Bulimia

Cycles of eating large amounts and then trying to compensate, often hidden behind shame and secrecy.

Binge eating disorder

Repeated episodes of eating with a feeling of loss of control, often followed by distress. It's the most common eating disorder.

ARFID

Avoiding or restricting foods because of sensory issues, fear of choking or low interest in eating — not because of body image.

OSFED

Serious eating disorders that don't fit one neat category. Just as real, and just as deserving of treatment.

Signs worth gently noticing

  • Constant preoccupation with food, eating or body shape
  • Skipping meals with others, or eating in secret
  • Rigid rules or rituals around food
  • Mood changes, anxiety or irritability around meals
  • Pulling away from friends and things once enjoyed

Where it can come from

There is rarely one cause. Eating disorders grow from many roots at once — and none of them are your fault.

Biology

Genes, temperament and brain chemistry all play a part. Eating disorders often run in families, the same way anxiety or depression can.

Mind

Anxiety, perfectionism, low self-worth and a need to feel in control often sit underneath. Food can become the place where those feelings show up.

The world around us

Diet culture, weight stigma, bullying about bodies, and pressure in sport or dance can all add weight to what someone is already carrying.

Sometimes, it starts with trauma

Many people living with an eating disorder have been through something painful — abuse, neglect, violence, bullying, grief, or living somewhere that never felt safe. Research has found that trauma and post-traumatic stress are common alongside eating disorders, especially bulimia and binge eating.

For some people, restricting, bingeing or purging becomes a way to cope: to numb, to feel in control, or to survive feelings that were too big to hold. That isn't weakness. It's a mind trying to protect you.

If this is part of your story, you are not alone, and it was not your fault. Trauma-informed therapists can help you heal both — the eating disorder and what's underneath it.

Sources: Brewerton, Eating Disorders, 2007 · Treasure et al., The Lancet, 2020

Some things we've been told aren't true

Tap a myth to see the truth.

  1. They're complex illnesses shaped by biology, psychology and environment. Nobody chooses one.

  2. Most people with eating disorders are not visibly underweight. Size tells you nothing about suffering.

  3. People of every gender, age, culture and background are affected — including men, older adults and children.

  4. If it's affecting your life, it's serious enough. You don't need to be "sick enough" to reach out.

  5. Many people fully recover. With support, healing is possible at any stage.

  6. Food is often where distress shows up. Underneath can be anxiety, control, trauma, grief or identity.

Twelve-step groups

Free, peer-led fellowships where people in recovery meet — online and in person, all over the world. Many find strength in not walking alone.

Eating Disorders Anonymous

For anyone with any eating disorder. Its focus is balance and moderation, not rigid rules. "The only requirement for membership is a desire to recover."

eatingdisordersanonymous.org ↗

Anorexics and Bulimics Anonymous

A fellowship for people recovering from anorexia, bulimia, compulsive overeating and related eating disorders. Free, anonymous meetings worldwide.

aba12steps.org ↗

Overeaters Anonymous

The longest-running food fellowship, for anyone with problems related to food — including binge eating, bulimia and anorexia.

oa.org ↗

Twelve-step groups are peer support, not treatment. Some meetings use language about "abstinence" or food plans that may not suit everyone, especially in restrictive eating disorders. It's worth talking it over with your treatment team.

People who get it

Reddit communities where people share wins, hard days and questions. Reading other people's stories can make the loneliness a little smaller.

Online spaces can sometimes include triggering posts. Read each community's rules first, use content warnings, and step away whenever you need to. They are not a substitute for professional help.

    When it's someone you love

    Start with care, not food

    Pick a calm, private moment. Use "I" statements: "I've noticed you seem stressed around meals, and I care about you." Talk about feelings and behaviour you've seen, not weight or appearance.

    Expect resistance — and stay

    Denial, anger or secrecy are common. That doesn't mean you failed. Let them know the door stays open and you're not going anywhere.

    Leave body and diet talk behind

    Skip comments on anyone's size, "good" or "bad" foods, and compliments about weight loss. Praise who they are, not how they look.

    Encourage professional help

    Offer to help find a doctor, therapist or dietitian experienced in eating disorders — or to sit with them while they call a helpline.

    Look after yourself too

    Supporting someone is heavy. Carer communities like F.E.A.S.T. and Beat's carer services exist for you as well.
